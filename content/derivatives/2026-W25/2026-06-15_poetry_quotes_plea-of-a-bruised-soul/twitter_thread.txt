Most people think showing up for someone in pain means saying the right thing.

It doesn't.

It means not saying the wrong thing — which is almost everything we've been taught to say:

We weren't raised to sit with pain. We were taught to solve it.

So when someone shows us their fracture, our instinct is to hand them cement.

Reframe. Name the lesson. Point toward the exit.

But sometimes there is no exit they're ready for.

"Time heals."
"At least..."
"You need to stop living in the past."

Every one of these comes from care.

Every one of them — in the wrong moment — makes the person feel more alone than before the conversation started.

The difference between witness and rescue:

Witness: I see that this is real. I am not leaving because of it.

Rescue: Let me make this something other than what it is, so we can both feel better sooner.

One stays in the room. The other implies you should be somewhere else by now.

Fixing gets you closure. Witnessing gets you nothing except the discomfort of being present inside someone else's reality with no guaranteed end.

That's why we default to advice.

Advice creates motion. Motion feels like progress. Progress means we can exhale.

But they need your presence — not your exhale.

I wrote a poem from that place.

The specific loneliness of having your pain redirected — not dismissed, but redirected — by someone who genuinely cares.

The last four lines:

"see me here,
exactly as I am,
and don't look away."

That's the whole ask.

If someone has ever sat with you in a room that didn't feel good to sit in — no reframe, no lesson, no quiet implication you should have moved on by now — you know it's rarer than it should be.

And if you've never had it: you deserved it then. You deserve it now.
