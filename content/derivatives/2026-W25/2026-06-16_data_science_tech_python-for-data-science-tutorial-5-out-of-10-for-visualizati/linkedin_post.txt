Your charts get judged before your model does — and nobody warns you about this going into your first data science role.

I learned this the wrong way. Six months of feature engineering, a clean pipeline, a 0.94 R² score — then I pasted the default matplotlib output into the deck. Gray background. Tiny fonts. The first comments weren't about the analysis. They were about how difficult the charts were to read.

Visualization isn't cosmetic. It's communication. And in Python, you have two tools that handle 95% of what you'll ever need: matplotlib for control, seaborn for speed.

They're not competing libraries. Seaborn is built on top of matplotlib — it gives you high-level plots that look good in 3–5 lines. Matplotlib gives you control over every pixel when seaborn's defaults aren't enough. The mental model I use: seaborn for the 80%, matplotlib for the last 20%.

There are five plot types that cover nearly everything in exploratory data analysis: distributions, relationships, categorical comparisons, correlations, and grouped distributions. Learn those cold and you'll be fast on any dataset.

The bigger unlock is combining both libraries in a multi-panel figure. Instead of presenting charts sequentially, you hand stakeholders a dashboard — four related views in one frame. The conversation changes. People stop reading individual numbers and start seeing the story emerge across all of them.

That's the shift worth chasing: from 'how do I make this chart' to 'what does this chart need to say.'
