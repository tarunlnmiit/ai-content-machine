---

Most of us were never taught to *be* with pain. We were taught to solve it.

So when someone shows us their fracture, our instinct is to hand them cement. Reframe it. Name the lesson. Point toward the exit.

But this poem doesn't want an exit. It wants witness.

And the difference sounds small — it isn't. Witness says: I see that this is real, and I'm not leaving because of it. Rescue says: let me make this something other than what it is, so we can both feel better sooner.

[PERSONAL_INSERT: After one of the hardest periods of my life, someone looked me in the eye and said — "You need to stop living in the past." They meant well. They were trying to help. But what I heard was: your grief has become inconvenient. I nodded. Changed the subject. And felt more alone afterward than I had before we started talking.]

The poem names that specific loneliness. Not the pain itself — but the loneliness of having your pain redirected.

---

Witnessing is harder than fixing because it requires you to hold someone's discomfort without resolving it.

When you fix, you get closure. When you witness, you absorb someone's reality and sit inside it with them — and there's no guaranteed end to that. That's why we default to advice. Advice creates motion. Motion feels like progress. Progress means we can exhale.

But the bruised soul doesn't need your exhale. They need your presence.

I've been on both sides. And I've gotten it wrong in both directions.

[PERSONAL_INSERT: Someone trusted me enough to say they were hurting, and advice just poured out of me — because silence felt uncomfortable. What I didn't realize was that my discomfort had become the center of the conversation. If I could go back, I'd say far less. I'd sit beside them, let the silence stretch, and trust that presence can be more generous than wisdom.]

[PAUSE]

The poem ends on *don't look away.* Not *tell me it gets better.* Just: look at me. Keep looking. That's the whole ask.

[BROLL: empty room, single window light, stillness]

---

Pain doesn't always want to be solved. Sometimes it wants to be true — fully, out loud, in company.

The hardest thing you can offer someone is your full, undivided presence in a room that doesn't feel good to sit in. No reframe. No lesson. No quiet implication that they should have moved on by now.

Just: I see you here. I'm not going anywhere.

And if you've ever had someone do that for you — do it fully, without flinching — you know it's rarer than it should be.

If you've never had it: you deserved it then. You deserve it now.

[PAUSE]
