[PALMIER_BG: clean desk, laptop half-open, soft daylight — a workspace, not a studio]

For ten years, my job has been finding the signal in the noise. Models, pipelines, dashboards — the whole data science circus. And for just as long, I've been doing something that has nothing to do with any of that. Writing. Novels I never finished. Poems I never showed anyone.

For most of that time, those two halves of my brain never spoke to each other. Then AI got good. And I found out the skill that makes someone good at data science — asking precise questions — is the exact same skill that makes someone good at using AI. Not the tool. The question.

[OVERLAY moment if wanted — "10 years of data science → 1 skill for AI"]

Here's the pattern I see constantly. Someone opens ChatGPT, types "write me a story" or "fix my code," gets back something bland and generic, shrugs, and decides AI is overhyped.

That's not the AI failing. That's a vague prompt getting a vague answer — the oldest rule in data. Garbage in, garbage out.

[BROLL: hands typing a short, vague prompt into a chat window, then a flat generic-looking response]

The people getting genuinely useful results aren't using a secret tool. They're using specific, well-structured prompts. They tell the AI who it is, what they want, what constraints matter, and what "good" looks like. That's the entire trick. Let me show you instead of telling you — three of the actual prompts I use, for three completely different parts of my life.

[PAUSE]

I'd been stuck on a novel idea for, embarrassingly, years. Here's the prompt that unstuck it.

[OVERLAY moment if wanted — full prompt text card: "Take this seed idea: [YOUR IDEA]. Generate 8 escalating 'what if' twists that raise the emotional stakes and make the story harder and more interesting. Rank them by how much they'd hook a reader."]

It doesn't write the book for me. It does something better — it hands me eight doors and lets me choose the one that makes my pulse jump. The choice, the taste, the voice: still mine. The AI just cleared the fog.

[PERSONAL_INSERT: I remember the exact evening I ran that prompt. I'd rewritten the same opening chapter four times over two years, convinced the problem was my writing. It wasn't. The problem was I'd never once asked for options — I kept asking the AI, and myself, to just "make it better," which is the same as asking for nothing at all. The first time I got eight ranked twists back, I picked one in about ten seconds. That's when I understood the block was never about talent. It was about never having asked a specific enough question.]

[PALMIER_BG: same desk, slightly different framing — a small visual beat to mark the transition]

The same principle works at my day job. Instead of pasting an error and praying, I now paste this first.

[OVERLAY moment if wanted — full prompt text card: "You are a pragmatic senior engineer. I'll share code and errors. Explain the root cause in plain terms, give the smallest correct fix, and point out any edge case or security issue I'm missing. Ask before assuming requirements."]

One paragraph of setup, and the assistant stops being a search box and starts being a colleague. My debugging time genuinely dropped — not because the model got smarter, but because I finally told it what role to play and what "done" looks like.

[PAUSE]

And because not every day is a productive one, here's a quieter prompt I keep for myself.

[OVERLAY moment if wanted — full prompt text card: "I'm feeling [WHAT YOU FEEL]. Ask me three gentle questions to help me separate the facts from the fears, then suggest one small, kind next step. Be warm and brief."]

It's not therapy. It's just a way to think on paper when your head is loud.

Notice what all three have in common. A role — "you are a senior engineer," "you are my writing partner." A specific ask — not "help me," but "give me eight ranked twists." Constraints — tone, length, what to avoid. And room for your own judgment — the AI generates options, you decide. That's it. That's the whole craft. Once you internalize it, free ChatGPT or Claude becomes shockingly capable.

[BROLL: the same vague prompt from earlier, now rewritten with a role, a specific ask, and constraints — noticeably more detailed]

I spent a decade believing the analyst and the writer were two different people who happened to share a skull. They're not. They're the same person asking good questions, just about different things. AI didn't replace either one of them. It just finally got them talking.

[PAUSE]

If you've got two halves of your own brain that never speak — the spreadsheet half and the daydreaming half — maybe this is the nudge. Ask a better question. See what comes back.
