# The One Skill That Makes You Good at AI (It's Not What You Think)

*I'm a data scientist who writes novels. The same skill powers both — and most people are missing it. Plus a few prompts you can steal.*

For ten years, my job has been to find the signal in the noise. Models, pipelines, dashboards, the whole data-science circus. And for just as long, I've been doing something that has nothing to do with any of that: writing. Novels I never finished. Poems I never showed anyone. Blog posts about, honestly, everything.

For most of that time, those two halves of my brain never spoke to each other. The analyst didn't help the poet. The poet definitely didn't help the analyst.

Then AI got good. And the strange thing I discovered is this: the skill that makes someone good at data science — asking precise questions — is the exact skill that makes someone good at using AI. Not the tool. The question.

**Most people use AI wrong (and then blame the AI)**

Here's the pattern I see constantly. Someone opens ChatGPT, types "write me a story" or "fix my code," gets back something bland and generic, shrugs, and decides AI is overhyped.

But that's not the AI failing. That's a vague prompt getting a vague answer — the oldest rule in data: garbage in, garbage out.

The people getting genuinely useful results aren't using a secret tool. They're using specific, well-structured prompts. They tell the AI who it is, what they want, what constraints matter, and what "good" looks like. That's the entire trick.

Let me show you instead of telling you.

**A prompt that beats the blank page**

I'd been stuck on a novel idea for, embarrassingly, years. Here's the prompt that unstuck it:

```
Take this seed idea: [YOUR IDEA]. Generate 8 escalating "what if" twists that raise the emotional stakes and make the story harder and more interesting. Rank them by how much they'd hook a reader.
```

It doesn't write the book for me. It does something better — it hands me eight doors and lets me choose the one that makes my pulse jump. The choice, the taste, the voice: still mine. The AI just cleared the fog.

**A prompt for the other side of my brain**

The same principle works at my day job. Instead of pasting an error and praying, I now paste this first:

```
You are a pragmatic senior engineer. I'll share code and errors. Explain the root cause in plain terms, give the smallest correct fix, and point out any edge case or security issue I'm missing. Ask before assuming requirements.
```

One paragraph of setup, and the assistant stops being a search box and starts being a colleague. My debugging time genuinely dropped.

**A prompt for the days the words won't come**

And because not every day is a productive one, here's a quieter prompt I keep for myself:

```
I'm feeling [WHAT YOU FEEL]. Ask me three gentle questions to help me separate the facts from the fears, then suggest one small, kind next step. Be warm and brief.
```

It's not therapy. It's just a way to think on paper when your head is loud.

**The pattern behind all three**

Notice what those prompts have in common:

- A role — "you are a senior engineer," "you are my writing partner."
- A specific ask — not "help me," but "give me 8 ranked twists."
- Constraints — tone, length, what to avoid.
- Room for your judgment — the AI generates options; you decide.

That's it. That's the whole craft. Once you internalize it, free ChatGPT or Claude becomes shockingly capable.

**Why I bottled it up**

After building dozens of these for myself — for drafting fiction, planning my week, reviewing code, writing poetry, getting through hard days — I realized I'd basically assembled a toolkit. So I cleaned it up and put it somewhere other people could use it, and called it Novel Prompt Co.

It's a small shop of AI prompt packs, each one tested and copy-paste ready: a romance novel writing system, a poet's prompt deck, an AI productivity system, a developer's AI prompt pack, and a mindful pages journal. They're priced low on purpose, because I wanted them to be the kind of thing you buy without thinking twice and actually use.

But here's the honest part: you don't need to buy anything to get the value of this post. Steal those three prompts above. Tweak the structure. Build your own. That's genuinely the whole method.

**The bigger thing I learned**

I spent a decade believing the analyst and the writer were two different people who happened to share a skull. They're not. They're the same person asking good questions, just about different things.

AI didn't replace either one of them. It just finally got them talking.

If you've got two halves of your own brain that never speak — the spreadsheet half and the daydreaming half — maybe this is the nudge. Ask a better question. See what comes back.
