# Ten Years Of Independence Taught Me Two Things I Blame My Parents For. I Can Only Write One.

I was twenty-six, standing in a German supermarket at 8pm, holding a jar of pickle I did not need, and I could not remember the last time I had spoken out loud to another human being. That was the day I understood what leaving home at eighteen had charged me — not in some poetic, seasoned-by-life way, but in the plain arithmetic of a decade: ten years of independence, two grievances I still carry against the people who raised me, and only one of them I can put into words.

This is the letter I would send backwards if the post office took that kind of mail. Not to warn my eighteen-year-old self off. He was going to go regardless — I admire the grit that got him out the door, and I would not take it from him. But he thinks he has priced this. He has not. He has priced the rent and the flights and the missed festivals. He has not priced the rest.

## The Rage I Was Sure Would Fade By Twenty-Five

At eighteen I assumed the fighting was a phase. My parents and I were locked in the standard-issue adolescent war — every conversation a border dispute, every suggestion an insult, every silence a verdict. I was certain distance would cure it. Move out, grow up, come back, and we would meet as adults.

Ten years later, the rage has not gone. It has changed shape and lowered its voice, but it is still in the room. And somewhere around year six I stopped treating that as a personal failure and started treating it as what it is: a structural problem neither side chose.

Here is the mechanism, and once you see it you cannot unsee it. Technology moved faster during my formative years than during their entire adult lives. Not "faster" in the marketing sense — faster in the sense that the distance between my parents' twenties and mine is not measured in years but in worlds. New avenues, new people, new information arriving at the speed of a thumb. I got exposed to all of it, and I sprinted. They could not keep that pace. So they will always be a few steps behind me, permanently, and every conversation we have carries that lag like a bad phone line.

That is not their fault. This is the part it took me a decade to say without a knot in my throat: they spent every ounce of time and energy they had on providing for me. There was nothing left over to run my race beside me. You cannot build the road and also sprint down it.

Understanding this did not dissolve the anger. That is the honest reporting. It made the anger legible. Some things you don't get to resolve — you get to stop mistaking them for someone's malice.

## The One Blame I Can Actually Write Down

Still. I blame them for two things, and this is the first.

If they had not gotten me this educated, I would have been happy.

Read that again, because it took me years to be willing to write it and I have never found a way to make it sound less ungrateful than it is. Give me a smaller education and I would have been content with mundane things. A steady job. A street I knew. A life measured in Sundays. Peaceful. Mediocre in the technical sense of the word — the middle — and genuinely, uncomplicatedly happy.

Instead they bought me the good education. And the good education did what it was supposed to do: it showed me every other life that existed. My parents gave me an education expensive enough to show me every life I could have had, and not one thing to do with the seeing.

That is the trade nobody warns you about, because everyone selling education is selling the upside. Education is not capability alone. It is exposure. And exposure is irreversible. Once you have met the person who took a year off to paint in Lisbon, once you have sat next to the colleague who left a good salary to build something stupid and beautiful, you cannot go back to a life where those were not options. The door does not close. It stands open behind you for the rest of your life, letting in a draft.

I am ten years into wrestling with this and I do not have a solution. I want to be clear about that, because self-development writing has a bad habit of manufacturing a resolution in the last paragraph. I have not found one. If my future self finds it, he will write to us both.

## Ambition Is Not A Trait. It Is Something You Catch From People.

Circle back to that first blame, because it explains everything downstream.

At eighteen I thought my dream was to be a computer scientist. It was not a dream. It was the only shape available.

My one role model was a cousin studying to be a computer scientist. His parents were much like mine — good people, fully occupied with providing, with no bandwidth left for exploration. So nobody had shown him another path either. And nobody, in my entire childhood, ever put art in front of me. Not once. Not as a career, not as a life, not as a thing a real adult could do on purpose. So I "chose" the single visible option and called it a calling.

Then I got educated. I moved out. And I met people carrying a whole plethora of ambitions I had never been offered — and I caught them like a fever. That is the accurate verb. Not "developed." Caught. Ambition ran through me as a contagion I picked up from proximity to people whose parents had shown them more doors.

So I started chasing things I had never dreamed of, for the reason that I had never been given the raw material to dream them.

I am still chasing them. And every time I feel I am getting close, something moves the finish line further out.

If you are eighteen and reading this: your dream is not a dream. It is an inventory. It is a list of everything you have been shown, and you have mistaken the length of the list for the size of the world. That is not a character flaw. It is what happens when the people who love you were too busy keeping you alive to also take you to a gallery.

## The Blame I Cannot Write, And What It Did To The Rest Of My Life

The second thing I blame them for, I cannot talk about.

Not because it is a secret. Because I don't want to live that pain yet, and writing it is living it. You will get there when you get there, and it will shatter you, and it will detach you from them, and it will not let you be made whole again. I am not going to dress that up. There is no lesson at the end of it that I have found.

So instead let me tell you what the whole decade built out of me, since that is the part I can hand over.

The engine underneath all the ambition turns out not to be ambition. It is love, in all its forms — the zeal for new things, the hunger for some standing among your peers, lost love, unrequited love, heartbreak. All of it is love wearing a work costume.

You will give love to the world with open arms. It has a caveat only experience teaches: more often than not, it does not come back. That does make you stronger, eventually, though I would not use that as a reason to volunteer for it. It also brings self-doubt and self-criticism, and I let them into my head, and I almost lost my existence in this world twice. I am not going to be more specific than that. I tell you only because I want you to know that the walls you will build are not paranoia — they are a repair.

And that same longing is what made me a writer. It started as lyrics for songs that did not exist. It became poems. Then a heartbreak, bad in the worst available way, became a novel. We are not a published author yet — but close enough to say the word out loud without flinching. People read the things I put online. More than a thousand of them followed. Some of them paid. At eighteen you would not have believed that sentence.

I worked as a computer scientist in India and hit a wall. I wanted out; I wanted to write. I raised it with our parents and it went nowhere. So I moved to Germany to study further, carrying a quiet second agenda — to take the writing seriously. I did. I have written more here than in my entire life before it, and travelled across Europe on top of that. That is a real win and I am not going to bury it under the rest.

Here is what stayed constant underneath all of it: I have spent most of my waking hours with a feeling of loneliness. I built walls I would call impenetrable if a few people had not walked through them anyway. Some of the ones I let in brought chaos, and I learned — slowly, expensively — to let those monsters go. And a precious few kept the sanctity of the castle intact. That is the whole ledger. Not many. A few. It turns out a few is enough, and nobody tells you that either.

## What Ten Years Of Freedom Actually Costs

So — was it worth it?

Wrong question. That question assumes there was a version of me that could have stayed. There wasn't. The kid was going. The only thing on offer was whether he would understand the invoice when it came.

Here is the invoice, itemised, for anyone standing at the door with a bag: independence does not cost you your family. It costs you the version of yourself that could have been satisfied. You trade a life you would have enjoyed for a life you can respect, and you do not find out which one you preferred until you are too far in to test it.

I would still go. That is the part that unsettles me most. Knowing the loneliness, the two blames, the finish line that keeps moving — I would still walk out that door at eighteen, because the alternative is a peace I would have had to stay small to keep.

I don't have a solution for you. I have a name for it, which is less than you wanted and more than I had at eighteen.

They gave me the education that made the small life impossible, and their love was the only reason they could afford it.

## If This Landed Somewhere

If you left home young and have spent years failing to explain to anyone why you are both grateful and furious — you are not broken and you are not ungrateful. You are holding two true things that refuse to reconcile.

Send this to the one person who left at the same time you did. The one you have never quite managed to say this to. You don't have to write anything with it.

And if you are still looking for the solution to the first blame, tell me. I have been looking for ten years. I will take a lead from anywhere.
