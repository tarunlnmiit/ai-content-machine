# Stop Trusting Your Gut: The Case for Making Decisions Under Pressure Without It

*Your gut reads the size of the number, not the shape of the work — and under pressure that's exactly when it fails you.*

A few years ago I got the offer I'd been quietly waiting my whole career for: more money, a flashier title, a team that would report to me. My gut screamed *take it — this is the validation you deserve.* I turned it down, and it was one of the best decisions I've ever made.

That's the thing nobody tells you about **making decisions under pressure**: the loudest voice in the room is the least reliable one. "Trust your gut" gets passed around like timeless wisdom, but the moments when we lean on it hardest — the high-stakes, adrenaline-soaked, decide-now moments — are the exact moments it's least equipped to help.

Here's what happened with that offer. On the surface it was everything I wanted. But instead of riding the rush, I made myself write down the honest reasons for each side. On paper, the new role was a lateral move dressed up in a raise — the growth I wanted lived where I already was, in a model I'd just gotten real ownership of. Within eight months, that project became the thing that defined the next stage of my career. The flashy offer would have buried me in management I wasn't ready for and didn't want. My gut was reading the *size of the number*, not the *shape of the work*.

That gap — between what feels compelling and what's true — is where instinct quietly betrays you. And it betrays you worst when emotion is running high, which, not coincidentally, is precisely when the pressure is on.

## The number that made it undeniable

Two years ago I almost hired someone my gut loved. Sharp in the interview, great energy, said all the right things about our stack. Every instinct told me to make the offer.

Instead I ran the same scored rubric I use for every candidate: a weighted sheet, a take-home task graded blind, reference checks converted to numbers, a hard cutoff at 75. He came in at 61 — mostly because the take-home showed he couldn't debug his own code without hand-holding. I passed. I felt bad about it for a week.

Then do the math on what instinct was walking me toward. A bad senior data-science hire in my market runs roughly ₹18–22 lakh in salary before you can cleanly exit them, plus three or four months of my time managing the mess and re-running the search. Call it a ₹20 lakh mistake the rubric caught. The person we hired instead — rank two, the one I found *less* exciting in the room — is still on the team and shipped our forecasting rewrite.

My gut was measuring charisma. The sheet was measuring whether they could do the job.

## Why "just slow down" is a fantasy

The popular fix for bad snap decisions is to slow down and think clearly in the moment. It sounds wise. It doesn't work.

Under pressure, your prefrontal cortex — the part that does careful reasoning — is the first thing to go offline. So "be more deliberate right now" is asking the exact part of your brain that just left the building to run the show. You will not feel the adrenaline spike and calmly choose to breathe your way to a good decision. That's a story we tell ourselves afterward.

The deliberate work has to happen *before* the moment. For the situations I know will spike me — a production model breaking during a live demo, a stakeholder challenging a number in front of the room — I keep a written pre-mortem. When it actually happens, I'm not thinking. I'm executing a decision I already made when I was calm.

Slowing down in the moment is a fantasy. Front-loading the thinking is the real version. My whole operating principle fits in one line I stole from somewhere and never gave back: *hope for the best, prepare for the worst.* The preparation is where the calm comes from.

## The mistake that rewired how I decide

I didn't learn any of this from a book. I learned it the expensive way.

Early in my career I shipped a churn model my gut swore was right. The features *felt* predictive. The validation numbers looked clean. I trusted that feeling instead of checking how the data was split — and it turned out I'd leaked future information into the training set. The model was quietly reading tomorrow's answer off today's row. It looked brilliant in testing and fell apart the week it went live. I spent three days in front of a stakeholder explaining why the thing I'd promised would cut churn did exactly nothing.

What it cost me wasn't only credibility. It was the comfortable belief that experience earns you the right to skip the boring check. My gut hadn't been reading the problem. It had been reading my own wish that the work be done.

So now, under pressure, I don't ask *what does this feel like.* I ask a different question:

**What would I have to see to know I'm wrong — and then I go look for that.**

That single reframe does more than any breathing exercise ever has. The feeling of certainty isn't a signal that you're right; it's the exact thing that fails you when the stakes are highest. Certainty feels the same whether you're correct or catastrophically fooling yourself. The only way to tell them apart is to go hunting for the evidence that would break your own case — before reality does it for you.

None of this means intuition is worthless. It means intuition is an input, not a verdict. On a quiet Tuesday, follow your gut wherever you like. But when the clock is ticking and the emotion is high — when it matters most — don't ask it to make the call. Ask it what it's afraid of, write that down when you're calm, and let the calm version of you decide for the panicked one.

The best decision I make under pressure is almost always one I already made yesterday.
