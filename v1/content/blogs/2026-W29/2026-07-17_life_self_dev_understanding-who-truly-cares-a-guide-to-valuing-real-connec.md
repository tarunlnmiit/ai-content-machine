# The Person You're Begging To Notice You Isn't The Problem. This Is.

## HOOK

I scrolled up through a chat thread and counted. Forty-one messages from me. Nine from him — six of which were a thumbs up.

I do this for a living. Ten years of looking at data and asking what it's actually saying instead of what I want it to say. And I had somehow never once run the numbers on my own friendships.

So I opened another thread. One I hadn't touched in three weeks. My friend had asked how my week went. Twice. Then stopped asking.

That's when it landed. I hadn't been unlucky in who I chose. I'd been running a broken model — optimizing hard for the one signal that gave me nothing, and throwing away the signal that was quietly correct the entire time.

"Him" was someone from a coding bootcamp cohort I did back in 2016 — funny, sharp, the one everyone wanted at the table. We'd built what felt like real chemistry over eight months of messages. Forty-one from me. Nine from him. Six of the nine, a thumbs-up emoji.

## CONTEXT

Here's the thing nobody says out loud about one-sided relationships: the pain isn't really about the person ignoring you.

You already know they're not choosing you. You've known for a while. You've built an entire private research practice around it — re-reading their last message for tone, timing your replies, noticing that they posted a story eleven minutes after leaving you on read. You are not confused. You're *committed*.

And the whole time, there are two or three people in your life who have been consistently, unglamorously present. Who showed up when your dad was in the hospital. Who remember the name of the project you were stressed about in March.

You're not ignoring them because you don't love them. You're ignoring them because they don't create the sensation you've learned to read as love: the sensation of *not being sure*.

That's the mechanism. And once you see it, the whole thing reorganizes — because it means the person you're chasing was never really the variable that mattered.

---

## SECTION 1

### Your Brain Doesn't Want Them. It Wants the Unresolved Ending.

There's a reason unavailable people feel more valuable, and it has nothing to do with them being more valuable.

Anything that seems just out of reach gets a premium attached to it. Their approval costs more, so we price it higher — the same way we assume the expensive restaurant is the good one. The scarcity *is* the appeal. Their attention feels like a scored achievement instead of a given, and achievements light you up in a way that gifts don't.

So the chase isn't about them. It's about the thrill of the chase. The story that hasn't finished yet.

Watch what happens when one of these people finally does turn toward you. The ones who cave and start texting back — do they stay interesting? Usually not. The heat drops out of it within weeks, and you're left holding a person you don't actually have much to say to. Because the thing you wanted was never their company. It was the resolution of an open loop.

That's an uncomfortable thing to sit with, because it means the six months weren't about love. They were about a question mark.

It happened with a job once. A company I'd been trying to get noticed by for two years — cold emails, conference small talk, the whole quiet campaign — finally called back. I took the interview. Ninety minutes in, I realized I didn't actually want the job. I wanted the callback.

And here's the cruel part: the loop can't close. Not through effort. There is no message good enough, no achievement impressive enough, no version of you patient enough. You cannot earn your way into someone's priorities. Priorities aren't a puzzle. They're just a fact about where someone's attention already lives.

---

## SECTION 2

### Real Care Is Boring, Which Is Exactly Why You Miss It

Genuine connection has almost no signal spike. That's the problem.

It shows up as: they make time. They check in periodically. They offer a hand before you ask. They don't announce it, don't make a moment of it, don't leave you wondering. It's low-variance. Steady. And your nervous system, tuned to notice change, reads *steady* as *nothing happening*.

So the person who has been present for eight years generates no data point. And the person who took six hours to reply generates a whole afternoon of analysis.

You are not broken for this. You're just measuring the wrong thing. You're measuring intensity when you should be measuring consistency.

*two friends laughing together on a sofa in a living room — Photo by [Jeff Vinluan](https://www.pexels.com/photo/two-women-sitting-on-couch-while-talking-10549067/) on Pexels*

Which brings up the only reliable test I know: **words are cheap, actions are the only real data.** If you're unsure about someone's intentions, stop analyzing their intentions. Wait, and watch what they actually do over time.

People will tell you they care and mean it in the moment. Meaning it and doing it are different variables. The person who says "we should catch up soon" for two years and the person who just shows up at the hospital are not describing the same relationship — they're describing two different ones, and only one of them exists.

Care that has to be interpreted usually isn't care. Care that's real tends to be embarrassingly obvious in hindsight. The tell isn't warmth in someone's language. It's whether they were there when it was inconvenient.

A friend once drove forty minutes to drop off a laptop charger I needed for a client call the next morning. Didn't make a thing of it — just texted "left it with your building guard." I didn't think about that text again for two years, not until I was doing this audit and it was the first thing that surfaced.

## SECTION 3

### The Cost Isn't the Rejection. It's What Happens Behind Your Back While You're Being Rejected.

Everyone frames this as a self-esteem story. You chased, you got hurt, you learned. Sad, but contained.

It isn't contained.

While you were pointed in the wrong direction, the people who were actually rooting for you kept extending. They asked how you were doing. They invited you to things. They noticed you were off and asked once, gently. And you gave them the leftovers — half-present, distracted, waiting for a different phone to buzz.

They felt that. Of course they felt that. Being someone's second-tier priority is not subtle from the inside — you know exactly what it's like, you've been studying the feeling for months.

So they extend a little less. Then a little less. Not out of punishment. Out of self-respect. And eventually the thread goes quiet and you tell yourself people drift apart, life gets busy, it's nobody's fault.

[QUOTABLE] I had spent six months auditing one person's silence for meaning, and zero minutes noticing that my friend had shown up for every single thing without ever being asked.

That's the actual bill. Not the humiliation of chasing — the friendship you let quietly starve while you were busy. Chasing the unavailable doesn't just cost you. It costs the people who were good to you, and they pay it without ever telling you they're paying it.

Nobody grieves the friendships they abandoned while chasing. They just disappear, and we call it drift.

There was a stretch during a brutal project deadline where a close friend kept inviting me to things — a birthday, a Sunday cricket match, just coffee — and I kept saying "next time." Six "next times" later, the invites stopped. I told myself he got busy. He didn't. I did.

## SECTION 4

### The Real Problem Isn't Them. It's That Their Approval Is the Only Metric You Trust.

Here's the part I resisted the longest.

Cutting the unavailable person out doesn't fix this. I know, because I've done it, and then found a new one within four months. Different name, identical dynamic. If the pattern regenerates, the person was never the cause — they were just the current hire for a role that was already open.

The role is: **the external judge who decides whether you're enough.**

If your sense of your own worth is downstream of someone else's approval, you will always be drawn to the person who withholds it — because their approval is the only kind that would *prove* anything. Approval from people who already love you doesn't count. It's not evidence, it's just affection. You discount it precisely because it's freely given.

So you go looking for a hard grader. And then you're devastated when they grade you low, and you read the grade as a verdict on your value.

It isn't. It's a mismatch of priorities. That's all it ever was. **Not being someone's priority is information about their priorities, not information about your worth.** Those two things feel absolutely identical from the inside, and they are not remotely the same.

Which means the work isn't out there. It's the unglamorous internal stuff — the journaling, the honest self-talk, the sitting still — that slowly builds a sense of your own worth that doesn't require a co-signer. Not so you stop needing people. So you stop needing a *verdict* from them.

And the strange thing that happens when the judge's chair is empty: the people who were always there stop being background. You can finally see them. They didn't change. Your instrument did.

What actually moved the needle wasn't a framework. It was an unglamorous habit: writing down one thing at the end of each week that had nothing to do with output — a conversation, a kindness, something that made me feel steady. Three months into that list, the names on it stopped surprising me. Same two or three people, every single week.

## TAKEAWAY

Letting go of a one-sided connection isn't bitterness. It's not coldness, and it isn't a punishment you're issuing. It's just the moment you stop asking a person to be something they've already told you, repeatedly and clearly, that they're not.

It also isn't a purge. You don't need to delete anyone. You need to move where your emotional energy goes — which is a quieter, less dramatic act than cutting someone off, and considerably harder.

Try the thing I should have done years ago instead of counting messages. Take one person who has been consistently good to you — the low-variance one, the boring signal, the one who has never once made you anxious — and tell them specifically what they did that you noticed. Not "thanks for everything." The actual thing. The Tuesday they called.

You'll feel how differently that lands compared to a reply you waited nine hours for.

The people who love you are not a waiting room. They will not sit there forever holding a number.
