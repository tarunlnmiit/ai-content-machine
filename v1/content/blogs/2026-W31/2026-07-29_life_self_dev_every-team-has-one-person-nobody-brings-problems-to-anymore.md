# There's a One-Word Test That Tells You If People Have Stopped Bringing You Problems

*Every team has one person nobody escalates to anymore. The tells are small, quiet, and easy to read as peace.*

"Yeah, we've been correcting for that manually since March."

Since *March*. I was three years into the job, half-reading a thread where two analysts were debugging a downstream dashboard, and one of them typed that as an aside — the way you mention weather. A feature in a pipeline I owned had been broken for six weeks. Nobody told me. The fix took two days. What I sat with wasn't the bug. It was that three competent people had built a workaround rather than walk ten feet to my desk. That's not a data problem. That's the moment you learn people stopped bringing you problems, and the reason is something you did the last few times they brought you one.

Every team has this person. They're usually competent — often *the* most competent — and they almost never find out, because being routed around feels exactly like things going smoothly.

## The tense test

The signal I trust most is the grammar of the questions I get.

When people are still bringing me problems, questions arrive in future or conditional tense. *Should we cut the cohort this way? What happens if we exclude the trial accounts?* The decision hasn't been made. I'm inside it.

When I'm being routed around, they arrive in past tense. *We went with the 30-day window, FYI.* The decision is already in the deck; I'm being informed so nobody can say I wasn't. Same friendly tone, same person, completely different position in the process.

The noisier signals are real too — getting added to a thread at message fourteen, meeting invites where the analysis is finished and you're there to nod. But tense is the cheapest one to check, and you can audit a week of it in ten minutes.

## The small problem is a test, and it's timed

Year seven, a PM asked me to sanity-check a date filter. Tiny question. I was mid-model, so I fired back a clipped two-line Slack reply and went back to work. Efficient.

The real question underneath — how to define an active user for the entire quarterly review — went to someone three desks away. I found out when I saw the number in a slide I hadn't reviewed.

That's what the small question is. It isn't someone being polite about your time. It's someone checking whether you're safe to bring the big one to. They float something low-stakes, watch what it costs them, and decide. I failed that test in about forty seconds without noticing I was being given one.

## "Be more approachable" fixes the wrong thing

An open-door policy fixes the door. But people don't stop escalating because they couldn't get in. They stop because of what the last visit cost them.

There was a lead I sat next to who was genuinely lovely — remembered your kid's name, always said *come find me anytime*. By month four, nobody brought him anything. Every problem you handed over came back as a two-week review with three new stakeholders attached. The door was open. The room had a toll booth.

I was that person myself for about a year, in a different flavor. Someone would bring me a broken pipeline and I'd explain — warmly, patiently, at length — why the design was correct. Then they'd quietly patch it in their own branch and stop mentioning it.

So don't audit your warmth. Warmth is not the variable. Audit the last five problems someone brought you: how long did each one take to close, and did the person who raised it ever find out how it ended?

## The mechanism (it's less dramatic than you think)

Around year six I was the data-quality person on my team. Someone would come to me with a number that looked off, and before they finished the sentence I was already typing — pulling the query, checking the join. *Wait, back up, which run is this? Did you check the upstream table first?* And the closing line was almost always some version of *this would've been caught earlier if the check was in place.*

No sigh. Nothing you could screenshot. I thought I was being helpful, because I *was* solving it. What I was doing was making someone stand there while I did their thinking out loud faster than they could follow. I never once opened with "ugh, good catch, thanks."

The tell was quiet. A colleague started prefacing things with "sorry, I know you're busy, but—" and a month later she stopped coming at all, routing the same questions to a guy two desks over who was slower but easier. I only noticed because I saw the thread and thought, *why didn't she ask me? I'd have had it in five minutes.*

That's the whole answer. Five minutes of my speed cost more than an hour of his patience.

People don't stop bringing you problems because you yelled. They stop because your first reaction was a question that sounded like a cross-examination, and they learned that bad news costs them a defense.

## The repair that works, and the one that backfires

The backfire is announcing it. Standing up in a team meeting to say your door is always open asks people to trust a claim instead of watching for evidence — and it quietly signals that you already know and would like credit for knowing.

The version that works is smaller and has no applause in it. Ask two or three people something specific: not "any feedback for me?" but *"the last time you brought me something half-finished, what did I do that made it not worth it?"*

Then, the next time someone brings you something rough, shut up and ask questions for the first ten minutes.

I did this badly for about two years. What changed it wasn't a resolution, it was one review where I sat on my hands and asked "what have you already tried?" — and then waited through the silence. The person came back the next week with something messier and earlier.

That's the metric. Not whether people call you approachable. Whether the drafts they bring you get rougher over time.

## When the silence is fine

Sometimes people have stopped wasting your time, and that's the correct outcome. The test isn't how often problems reach you — it's the *state* they're in when they arrive.

After I documented our pipeline failure modes, people stopped pinging me about broken DAG runs. Good. I'd removed myself from a queue I never should have been in. If people have stopped bringing you the solved-shaped problems but still bring you the ugly half-formed ones — the "I can't even describe what's wrong yet" ones — you're fine. They got better.

If everything that arrives is already decided, and your role is to nod at a number in a slide, you're not efficient. You've been routed around politely.

The six weeks were never about the pipeline. Somebody typed *since March* into a thread, and it landed like a receipt for how I'd been answering all year.
